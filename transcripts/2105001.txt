Rule-aware behavior:

- Group input chunks by document and canonical page order.
- Start structural units at Bengali/Latin numbered rules such as `[২.১১১]`, subsection labels such

- Keep definition text with following examples introduced by markers such as `যেমন`, `উদাহরণ`, or

REFLECTION
==========

WHAT I ACCEPTED
---------------

I accepted the deterministic layout baseline because the books are mostly
single-column grammar text with headings and ruled tables. The real-page
gallery showed that projection-based blocks and atomic table detection were
adequate without adding a large learned layout model. I also accepted retaining
the isolated box around a printed year because the year is real source content;
a page-specific removal rule would make the layout less general.

I accepted EasyOCR `bn+en` as the operational OCR reader because it offered the
best speed, accuracy, dependency cost, and reproducibility tradeoff for a
783-page Kaggle build. Its clean NCTB accuracy was strong, it processed the full
corpus in one reproducible pipeline, and its weaknesses on historical pages
were measured rather than hidden. NFC-only normalization was accepted because
it preserves meaningful Bangla distinctions better than compatibility
normalization.

I accepted rule-aware chunking as the selected strategy. A Bengali grammar
answer needs the numbered rule or subsection, its definition, its examples,
and its canonical citation pages together. The rule-aware method preserves
these units, keeps tables atomic where possible, carries every contributing
page ID, and uses overlapping windows only when a structural unit is too large
or no reliable rule boundary exists.

I accepted the separation between local Git commits and Kaggle execution.
Source and tests were committed first, Kaggle cloned and printed the exact
source commit, and the downloaded output-bearing notebook received its own
later evidence commit. This keeps both authorship and measurements auditable.

WHAT I CHANGED
--------------

I changed the initial idea of enabling Qwen3-VL 8B as a full historical-book
fallback. The stored Phase 3 candidate improved historical held-out OCR, but
running it over all 565 historical pages would add substantial runtime, memory,
and dependency cost. I kept it as a measured comparison and selected EasyOCR
for the operational full build.

Testing also changed parts of the implementation. I corrected zero-padded page
ID handling, replaced total-row-ink table detection with longest contiguous
stroke detection, and filtered nested duplicate regions after real-page
inspection. These changes were accepted only after the focused tests and
visual evidence supported them.

WHAT I REJECTED
---------------

I rejected plain fixed-size chunking as the final standalone chunking strategy
because a 256-token boundary can separate a grammatical definition from its
examples or rule number. I did not remove it: it remains the required baseline
and the fallback for oversized or unstructured text, allowing the next phase
to compare retrieval quality fairly against rule-aware chunks.

I rejected using native PDF text as OCR evidence, using NFKC normalization, and
silently correcting uncertain Bangla text. Those choices could create clean but
unfaithful grammar rules and break citation traceability.

I rejected implementing embeddings, FAISS persistence, and final retrieval in
my work package. Those tasks belong to the remaining portion of Phase 7 and
should be implemented, measured, and committed by the teammate who performs
them rather than being bundled into my contribution without evidence.
